# Measure rugs before GodsEye risks SOL

**Bottom line.** The rug that matters for GodsEye is not the textbook liquidity pull. In the largest independent Solana measurement, **79% of flagged rug tokens were sellable pump-and-dump cliffs, 20% were liquidity withdrawals and only 0.6% were freeze-authority abuse**, and the median creation-to-first-profit-taking time was well under an hour ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24625), H1 2025, Orca/Raydium/Meteora pools only). Pump.fun's structure removes the classic LP pull for migrated tokens, because the program burns the LP tokens at migration ([Pump program README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pump-fun/pump-public-docs/main/docs/PUMP_PROGRAM_README.md)). What is left for a bot that copies insider-pattern wallets in a token's first minutes is soft rugs: creator, bundle, sniper and insider-cluster dumps, plus a thin tail of token-program traps and the migration no-sell window. GodsEye cannot yet say how exposed it is. **It has never bought a token (0 positions, 741 skipped copy attempts), its rug screen has never run live (0 rows in `rug_checks`), and its swap tape cannot see rugs that end in silence.** The tape does show that **16.5% of 478 would-be copies fell 50% within an hour and 14% had a leader sell at half price or worse**, both lower bounds. No threshold in the current screen is validated, there is no sell simulation, and once a position is open nothing watches it for a rug, so the first funded week could produce losses that go unrecorded. The first move is therefore measurement, not new blockers: shadow-log every candidate with raw features and later outcomes from a source that sees every trade. That needs no SOL and fixes both the never-ran and the invisible-after-the-pull problems. Cheap token-property checks (Token-2022 extensions, a simulated buy-then-sell) and a post-buy unsellable/written-off state come next, each of them gated on what the shadow data shows. Insider-pattern wallets stay copyable throughout; everything here is about token risk.

Three readings of this table matter for the build. First, the 79/20/0.6 split describes pool-bearing tokens on Orca, Raydium and Meteora, and explicitly excludes pump.fun tokens that never created a pool. It should not be read as the mix on pump.fun curves, where the LP-pull route does not exist. Second, Chen's labels are conservative lower bounds on a 24 h window, and its own sample shows slow rugs hiding outside it, so any "within an hour" statistic is biased toward fast rugs. Third, there is **no source that splits dollar loss between hard and soft rugs, and none that measures what a one-minute-late copier actually experiences**; the claim that a copier mostly suffers a sellable 70-95% drawdown is inference from curve mechanics, not a finding.

The notes also supply the structural facts that decide which rugs are live for GodsEye. Pump.fun's `migrate` instruction is permissionless and burns the resulting LP tokens, and the PumpSwap docs define no pool-level admin that can withdraw funds ([PumpSwap README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pump-fun/pump-public-docs/main/docs/PUMP_SWAP_README.md)). Fewer than 2% of launches graduate ([Solana Compass](https://solanacompass.com/news/pumpfun-launched-42000-tokens-in-one-day-fewer-than-2-will-ever-reach-a-dex)), so the first-minutes universe is overwhelmingly curve tokens. What the notes could not establish is the authority state of `create_v2` mints. Legacy `create` mints use SPL Token; `create_v2` mints use Token-2022 with a metadata pointer, and the mint authority is initialised to a Pump PDA with no statement on whether it is later revoked ([COIN_CREATION](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pump-fun/pump-public-docs/main/docs/instructions/COIN_CREATION.md)). Token-2022 itself is therefore not a red flag for new pump coins, and a rule that blocks on any live mint authority could reject every `create_v2` launch.

## What GodsEye does today: a pre-buy screen that has never run

The only rug defence is a pre-buy screen inside `copyBuy` in `agent/src/copy/engine.ts`. After the non-memecoin filter, the 0.2 SOL leader minimum and the 60 s lateness limit, `roomFor` runs (including the wallet-balance check in real mode) and only then `rugCheck`, implemented in `agent/src/market/rugcheck.ts` and `holders.ts`. The screen covers mint and freeze authority, Token-2022 permanent-delegate, pausable and default-frozen extensions, RugCheck (fail-open, 10 s timeout), LP lock, bundle share, funder cluster, top-10 share and insider network. The six cutoffs are bundle 25%, cluster 15%, top-10 50%, insider 20%, locked LP 90% and RugCheck score 75, and **all of them are unvalidated defaults**. Verdicts are cached for 6 hours and stored as a latest-verdict-only upsert, so history would be overwritten even if the screen ran. The buy then goes through Jupiter at 300 bps slippage with a static check and a simulation of the buy.

The gaps are specific. There is no sell simulation, no minimum-liquidity gate and no token-age gate. There is no post-buy rug monitoring: exits are the 35% stop-loss, leader sells, a 48 h maximum hold and a 60 s price poll. Dead tokens with no price are skipped and stay open indefinitely, and failed sells are retried but never marked rugged. The copy-fill replay harness does not model rugs, so it cannot score any rug rule today. The screen also handles missing RugCheck pool data only as a warning ("liquidity lock unknown"), not as an explicit unknown state with a retry.

### What the internal audit can and cannot establish

The audit read `data/copycow.db` read-only over a 10-day tape (2026-10-16 to 2026-10-26, 2.86 M swaps). Its first finding is negative and complete: `positions` has 0 rows, `copy_trades` has 741 rows that are all skipped (464 because the wallet holds 0.000 SOL, 277 because the leader trade was too old), and `proof_ledger` is empty. Because `roomFor` fails first, `rugCheck` is never reached. That makes our own rug rate unanswerable, and it means the screen's latency, RPC cost and failure rate on minutes-old tokens are all unmeasured. No threshold can be judged because no pass-or-block history exists ([internal audit](../research_notes/Rug%20detection%20for%20copy%20trading/internal_audit.md)).

The second finding is a price-path study of 22,361 leader buys of at least 0.2 SOL by the 10 wallets with `copy_weight > 0`, on 877 mints. Taking the first leader buy per mint, with entry at the first stored swap 5-120 s later, leaves **478 modelled entries** (399 mints had no stored swap in that window). Of these, **16.5% fell at least 50% within 60 minutes, 1.3% fell at least 90%, and 14.0% had a leader sell at or below half the entry price**. PumpSwap-venue entries show 30.1% and 26.4% on the same two measures against 2.2% and 0.9% for venue-unknown entries, but the audit treats that split as probably confounded with token age and launch type. 340 of the 478 entries were on tokens under 5 minutes old at the leader's buy, the group the academic papers call riskiest.

What this cannot establish is the real rate. The tape holds only swaps by the 6,192 tracked wallets, so after a liquidity pull the price series just stops at the last pre-rug price. In 93.5% of entries the tape is silent within 60 minutes of entry (58% within 10 minutes), so "no drop seen" carries almost no information about survival. Entries also require a stored swap within 2 minutes, which drops 45% of mints, and untradeable tokens are exactly where honeypots would sit. The 16.5% and 14% are lower bounds, the 14% being the better of the two because leaders do sell into declines, though a bag-holding leader stays invisible. The literature figures (Chen's ~76%, MemeTrans's 84%) cannot be checked against this data. What the audit does justify is a practical conclusion: roughly one copy in six dropping by half in an hour is large relative to a 35% stop-loss, and a stop-loss only helps if the exit fills on a pool that may be drained.

## Chain reads make the gate; vendors give slow second opinions with thin accuracy evidence

Everything in the current hard-block list that is a property of the mint, its extensions, the pool or early holders can be read from RPC or a gRPC stream with no vendor. Vendors expose the same mint fields (GoPlus `mintable`, `freezable`, `transfer_hook`; Jupiter `audit.mintAuthorityDisabled`) as plain reads of the mint account ([GoPlus docs](https://docs.gopluslabs.io/reference/solanatokensecurityusingget), [Jupiter Tokens v2](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/tokens/v2)). What vendors add is labelled data: malicious-address tags, verified lists, organic score, entity clustering. The one thing the notes call impractical to rebuild is a curated scam-label database; the one thing no vendor sells is a creator-reputation table built from the bot's own observed launches.

The notes' verdict, which is an inference rather than a sourced finding, is to keep RPC checks as the gate and RugCheck fail-open, and to add two free second opinions in shadow mode. GoPlus Solana (beta) offers top-10 holders with lock detail, per-pool LP burn percentage and `*_upgradable` flags; its free tier is reported at 150K monthly units and 150 requests per minute on a Chinese-language vendor page ([GoPlus](https://gopluslabs.io/zh/security-api)), while the docs page states no limits. Jupiter Tokens v2 gives holders, verified status and organic score at 1 request per second with a free key and 100-mint batching ([Jupiter API setup](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/api-setup)). Bubblemaps has the only purpose-built cluster model (`time_nodes`, `magic_nodes`, `fresh_wallets`) but is credit-priced with no price found ([Bubblemaps OpenAPI](https://docs.bubblemaps.io/openapi.json)). SolSniffer, Token Sniffer and Birdeye security are not worth paying for before measurement, mainly because their docs could not be read and Token Sniffer's Solana coverage is disputed. Jupiter's `NOT_VERIFIED` and `NEW_LISTING` flags should never block anything, since every fresh memecoin carries them.

The accuracy evidence is weak. The only Solana tool comparison is in the Chen paper: RugChecker 76.92% precision and 94.02% recall, Solsniffer 67.45% and 97.44%, Solanatracker 98.96% and 81.89%, on 117 verified rugs plus 65 Jupiter-verified legitimate tokens ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24625v1)). Its authors built a competing tool, the negatives are blue-chip tokens rather than brand-new memecoins, and the paper does not say when each tool was queried. The 94% RugCheck recall most plausibly reflects flags raised after the rug, and the notes explicitly say it is not evidence of prediction at minute one. The one EVM-only tool assessment (SoK, 14 tools) found GoPlus averaging a 50% hit rate on combination cases and nine root causes missed by every tool ([SoK](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.16082v1)); no Solana benchmark of GoPlus, Birdeye, Bubblemaps or Jupiter Shield exists. No vendor publishes a time-to-first-report, and the notes treat vendor-derived signals as probably later than chain-derived ones, a hypothesis. Vendor snapshots also go stale: RugCheck-style scans are a point-in-time read ([Solana Tracker](https://www.solanatracker.io/blog/rugchecks)). The practical consequence is that vendor reports belong in a second stage at +1 to +5 minutes, not in a gate that must answer in seconds. Vendor terms were not read, so showing any vendor-derived score on the public site (read-only, redacted) needs a licence check first.

## Pre-buy additions are cheap, but the evidence says exit handling matters more

For the sell test, the notes rank four methods. A Jupiter sell quote prices only the AMM and cannot see a freeze, a reverting hook or a hidden tax, so it is a route-exists check, not a honeypot test. The static read of Token-2022 extensions is free because the mint is already fetched: the Solana docs show a transfer hook can fail every transfer ([Solana docs](https://solana.com/docs/tokens/extensions/transfer-hook)), the mint authority can update the hook after launch, a pending fee change is visible two epochs before it applies ([Neodyme](https://neodyme.io/en/blog/token-2022/), [Solana docs](https://solana.com/docs/tokens/extensions/transfer-fees)), and a permanent delegate can drain a vault. Rules must key on specific dangerous extensions (hook program, permanent delegate, pausable, non-transferable, default-frozen, confidential transfer, transfer fee current or pending), not on Token-2022 as such. One `simulateTransaction` of buy then sell from GodsEye's own wallet exercises the token program and shows the true round-trip loss; it must run from our wallet, since a leader's wallet may be allow-listed and give a false pass. A live test round trip costs at least about 2% on a curve token (1% each way per the docs) and adds nothing a simulation cannot show. How much this buys is bounded: freeze abuse is 0.6% of measured rugs, so for pump-native coins the simulation protects a minority tail (non-pump launchpads, Raydium/Meteora pools, unvetted `create_v2` extensions), and no dated Token-2022 hook honeypot on a pump coin was found.

The notes' exit-capacity arithmetic is more relevant to the dominant soft-rug case. For a 0.05-0.15 SOL ticket, own price impact on exit is under about 1.5% in any pool with 10 SOL or more of quote reserve, so a minimum-liquidity gate sized on GodsEye's own impact is easy to satisfy and protects little. What sets the real exit risk is how much supply the insiders hold relative to the pool: if a block of tokens T hits a constant-product pool with base reserve B, price falls by 1 - 1/(1+T/B)². Per that formula, T/B of 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 gives drops of 17%, 36%, 56%, 75% and 89%. At migration the pool holds about 206.9M tokens (the notes' derivation from the Global constants) and about 85 SOL, so insiders holding 5%, 10% or 20% of supply imply roughly 35%, 55% or 74% drops. This is the notes' arithmetic, not a measurement, and the suggested cutoff (reject above T/B of 0.5) is a design proposal. It reframes the existing bundle and top-10 checks as pool-relative, and it explains why a 35% stop-loss is often not enough once insiders sell first. Several pump-specific gates are free if the decoder keeps event fields: skip when `complete` is true with no migration seen (nothing can trade), skip or normalise any non-SOL `quote_mint`, exclude the Mayhem-mode agent's vault from concentration maths to avoid a false positive (untested), and make sure `create_v2` instruction variants are decoded at all, since a screen run on a decoder that misses them silently never fires.

Creator and leader context are cheaper still and are risk signals about a token, not verdicts on a wallet: whether the leader is the creator (`TradeEvent.user` equals `creator`), whether the leader bought within about five slots of the create, whether the creator has already sold. Published support is for the mechanism only; no study compares copies of creator-linked versus independent buyers. Creator history is weaker still. The numbers are vendor data (561,645 deployer wallets, 1.89% of launches bonded, [MadeOnSol](https://madeonsol.com/blog/pump-fun-deployer-analysis-2026-561k-wallets)) and one academic test that found creator conditioning did not improve prediction ([Marino et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14860v1)), so it earns a one-way veto or size-down at most.

## Post-buy: the exit machinery is the least built and the least evidenced part

No published study measures how many seconds before a collapse any on-chain precursor appears, the false-positive rate of any post-buy monitor, or realistic recovery after a pull. Everything below is design from the pool math, to be tested on shadow data. An LP removal is atomic, so the pull itself gives no lead time; lead exists only in precursors such as creator or insider-cluster selling, reserve drains in steps, or an authority change. A sell cannot beat a pull already in the stream, but it can beat the second wave of sellers. Because a proportional pull leaves the price ratio unchanged, the last-swap price can look normal while exit depth is gone: in the notes' example a position worth 1 SOL in a 100 SOL pool returns about 0.83 SOL after 95% of liquidity is removed and under 0.1 SOL after 99.9%. The most informative single observable is therefore the pool's vault balances, not the price feed. Jupiter's Price API v3 reports the last swapped price, omits tokens only after about 7 days without trades, and says nothing about rugged tokens ([Jupiter Price v3](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/price/v3)), so a dead token can keep a stale price for hours.

That is why the 300 bps slippage setting is a poor exit design. Slippage cannot create liquidity: proceeds are capped by residual reserves, and a frozen account, a disabled instruction or a blocking hook defeats any setting. The notes suggest computing the minimum-out from current vault reserves at send time and accepting a floor tied to the residual-depth formula. The open-source sniper that the notes examined handles the unsellable case by giving up on selling past a loss threshold ([fdundjer README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fdundjer/solana-sniper-bot/master/README.md)), which is the holding-the-bag behaviour GodsEye already approximates; no surveyed bot has a code-visible write-off. Jito can help only on a precursor, since conflicting bundles are ranked by tip within 50 ms auctions ([Jito docs](https://docs.jito.wtf/lowlatencytxnsend/)).

The proposed lifecycle is `active`, `degraded`, `unsellable`, `written_off`: degraded on stale price during expected activity or any trigger, unsellable after K failed sells or a simulated sell below a dust floor, written off at residual-depth value so the exposure slot frees up, with an hourly then daily re-check so a false write-off can be reversed. Tiered kill-switch triggers (vault drop of at least 50% in a few slots, freeze or authority events on the mint or GodsEye's own token account, creator or insider-cluster selling as hard triggers; reserve-implied price down 20-25% in under a minute or residual depth under 3x position as soft ones) are hypotheses; X = 50% is a starting guess. The base rate argues that even moderately precise triggers may pay on fresh tokens: MemeTrans finds 60.26% of migrated tokens below 20% of migration price within 20 minutes. A pre-filtered universe lowers that rate and makes false exits costlier.

### Thresholds and labels need GodsEye's own data

None of the six cutoffs has a published validated value. MemeTrans reports group-level effects (bundles raise top-10 share by about 24 points in high-risk tokens versus 6 in low-risk) and a headline loss reduction from 60.71% to 26.64% for a top-100 selection, but that comes from picking the safest ~1.5% of a 74%-high-risk population, so a veto-style filter should expect less ([MemeTrans](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13480v1)). The "Catching the Rug" study reports in-domain MCC of about 0.25-0.36 from first-5-minute features, near zero across venues, and describes itself as insufficient for deployment ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20271)). SolRPDS's AUC of 0.987 is circular because its label derives from the same features ([SolRPDS](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2504.07132)). The notes' calibration design is to log raw feature values before applying thresholds, split chronologically, report outcome by feature quantile, and block when P(rug|x) times the average loss exceeds the average gain on a non-rug, preferring one monotone score over six jointly tuned cutoffs. Blocked tokens are never bought, so the false-positive cost cannot be recovered from verdicts; credit-scoring reject inference says the only robust fix is to accept a random sample of rejected cases and observe them ([Hand and Henley, via HAL](https://hal-hec.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00655036v1)). By the notes' arithmetic, estimating a rug rate to ±5 points needs about 250-380 observations in the group, and a mean-return difference of 0.10 needs about 1,568 per arm assuming a return SD of 1.0 (an unverified assumption), so a single go/no-go on the whole screen takes roughly 4-10 weeks at 100 candidates per week or 1-3 weeks at 1,000.

For labelling, the notes recommend keeping layered columns rather than one flag: a hard-rug event (quote reserves down at least 90% from their running maximum within about 10 minutes, or at least 99% of LP removed), a collapse relative to GodsEye's entry (at most 30% within 20 minutes or at most 10% within 60), a dead flag (no swaps for N hours after a collapse; N of 1-6 h is a hypothesis), the bot-specific "unexitable" label (a simulated sell at actual position size returns under 10% of cost at any mark), and a 50-100 case manual audit slice. With reserve data, "no swap" is observable, so silence becomes an explicit outcome; without it, the case is "unknown", never "survived".

> **Corrections to the earlier report.** (1) The Solidus "98.6% of pump.fun launches" figure is a died-before-graduating metric, not a rug rate: it counts tokens with at least five trades that fell under $1,000 liquidity without migrating, which is consistent with a ~1.4% graduation rate; the page contains no time-to-rug or loss figure for pump.fun ([Solidus](https://www.soliduslabs.com/reports/solana-rug-pulls-pump-dumps-crypto-compliance)). Solidus's own pool count is also reported as 361,000 and 388,000 by different outlets. (2) Chen's lifecycle table contradicts its prose. Read as days, the table gives a median of ~11.6 h for freeze abuse and a P75 of ~23 h for liquidity withdrawal, while the text says medians are under an hour and P75 does not exceed 5 hours. The notes disagree on which table row feeds which number (the verified 117-rug benchmark has a median of ~17 min; the 76,469-token flagged set has ~35 min), so quote only "well under an hour for most types" until the released dataset resolves the units. (3) Kamat's persistent-cohort effect estimates (arXiv 2607.02795) are marked WITHDRAWN by the author pending a corrected analysis; only the cohort count and data scale remain usable ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02795)). (4) Chen's ~76% rug share excludes pump.fun-only tokens and is not a pump.fun rate. (5) A Chainalysis "74,037 pump-and-dump tokens" figure seen only in a search summary is unverified and should not be used.

Ranks reflect evidence strength first, then cost. "Evidence-backed" means the need is established by the audit or primary docs; "design" means the item is the notes' own suggestion and no study validates it. None of this is decided; it informs a later choice. Nothing here drops a wallet or adds a sniper tier, and nothing shown publicly changes the read-only, redacted policy.

## Conclusion

The notes change the question from "which rug checks to add" to "which rugs does GodsEye actually face", and the honest answer is that nobody knows yet, including GodsEye. The literature points to soft insider and bundle dumps for first-minutes pump.fun entries, and the one dataset GodsEye owns shows that roughly one in six modelled copies halves within the hour while being structurally blind to the worst cases. Because the screen sits behind a funding check that always fails, GodsEye has accumulated 741 skipped candidates without a single verdict, which is the cheapest evidence it will ever waste.

The implication is a sequencing rule: the first week of funding should not be the first week of measurement. Shadow logging turns the inactive screen into a labelled dataset, and exit-side machinery (reserve-based floors, an unsellable state, vault and creator monitoring) should exist before the wallet is funded, since an open position that cannot be priced or closed is a bookkeeping failure on top of a loss. Everything else on the ranked list is a hypothesis with a stated experiment, and the thresholds should be fitted last.
